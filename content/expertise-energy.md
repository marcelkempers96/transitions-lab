# Energy & Water Systems

*How energy and water systems are built, paid for and relied on, where the network reaches and where it never did.*

<p class="research-areas-strip"><strong>Research areas:</strong> Mini-grids and off-grid supply · Renewables, storage and grid integration · Water delivery, vendors and payment</p>

<!-- IMAGE energy-hero: hero photo | Solar mini-grid, home solar panel, water point or piped connection in daily use. Real, working, daylight. | Full-width hero under the standfirst. -->

Energy and water are the two systems everything else in a transition rests on. They are also the two where the same research question keeps arriving in different clothes: how does a metered, paid-for service reach a household the network was not built for, and stay reliable enough that people plan around it.

That is PAYGo solar in a village outside Kigali, and it is water vending on the edge of Nairobi. One research question asked twice.

---

**§ 1 / The energy market, in brief**

- [Sub-Saharan Africa accounted for **563 million** of the 655 million people without electricity in 2024](https://www.iea.org/reports/financing-electricity-access-in-africa); nearly **80% rural**.
- [Reaching universal access needs about **$15 billion a year**; under **$2.5 billion** was committed in 2023](https://www.iea.org/reports/financing-electricity-access-in-africa).
- [Mini-grids and standalone solar are among the **most cost-effective** ways to close the rural gap](https://trackingsdg7.esmap.org/).

**§ 2 / The water market, in brief**

- Globally, [**1 in 4 people (2.1 billion)**](https://www.who.int/news/item/26-08-2025-1-in-4-people-globally-still-lack-access-to-safe-drinking-water---who--unicef) still lack safely managed drinking water; **3.4 billion** lack safely managed sanitation (WHO/UNICEF JMP, 2025).
- In sub-Saharan Africa, [rural access sits at just **53%**](https://washdata.org/), and the region is the **only one** where the absolute number without access has grown.
- Women and girls bear the collection burden, many spending [**over 30 minutes a day** fetching water](https://www.who.int/teams/environment-climate-change-and-health/water-sanitation-and-health).

**§ 3 / What we see that others miss**

## In energy: the bottleneck is trust and delivery, not hardware

The technology is largely settled. Whether a household connects, keeps paying, and benefits depends on affordability, reliability, and whether the system was designed around how people actually use power.

The Lab documents that. We are the party asking whether locally-owned developers can scale, whether access converts into the productive uses that make it self-sustaining, and **who sits at the very end of the access curve**, the finding that decides if a transition is fair. Our [Pyropower case study](/case-pyropower) shows waste-to-energy at the smallholder scale.

Grid integration is the other half of this programme, and it is where the developed-market work sits. Generation assets, storage siting, curtailment and what happens on a grid that already exists are the questions a European utility or a critical minerals developer signing an anchor-tenant power contract has to answer, and they run through the same argument about who bears the cost of a shared system.

## In water: the failure is rarely the engineering

The technical solutions mostly exist. Success depends on whether infrastructure is sustained, trusted, and maintained, and whether it reaches the rural and marginalised.

The Lab documents why a hand pump is maintained in one village and abandoned in the next, what builds or erodes trust in a source, and whether sanitation behaviour actually changes beyond the counting of latrines. That is the socio-technical layer an engineering audit never reaches, and the one that decides whether investment lasts.

Our water expertise extends to the Dutch-language contexts of Suriname and Guyana, and to transboundary water systems where the technical meets the political. Water is where the Lab's roots run deepest: based in Delft, a global centre of water research, we pair genuine technical understanding of water systems with the field methods to study how they actually serve people.

**§ 4 / Topics we are following**

- What makes a household connect to a mini-grid, keep paying, and stay connected.
- How pay-as-you-go models land in a low-income household's real economics.
- Who decentralised energy and water services reach first, and who is reached last.
- What blocks locally-owned energy and water companies from scaling.
- Whether access translates into productive use, or stops at the lightbulb.
- Why water infrastructure is sustained in some communities and abandoned in others.
- What builds or erodes trust in a source, and how trust shapes use.
- How the collection burden falls on women and girls, and what genuinely shifts it.
- How sanitation behaviour actually changes.
- Grid integration, storage siting and curtailment on a grid that already exists.
- Anchor loads, coincident peak and who pays for firm capacity.
- Clean cooking, the most neglected part of the energy gap.
- Transboundary and climate-stressed water systems.
