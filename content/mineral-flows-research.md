## Starting point

This paper takes its cue from Ndubuisi, Avenyo, Owusu and Kassa's World Bank study, republished on 29 September 2026 by *The Conversation* and the South Africa Trade Desk. Their method runs in four steps.

First, they identify which transition minerals each African country holds. Second, they follow the trade data (CEPII BACI) to see who exports what to whom. Third, they place each country on the green-technology value chain according to whether it exports ore, processed material or components. Fourth, they review the national and regional policy attached to each stage.

They find deposits of at least one transition mineral in <a href="#fact-1" id="ref-1" class="fact-ref">forty-eight African countries</a>, report that most exports leave raw or lightly processed, and that Asia and China in particular take over <a href="#fact-2" id="ref-2" class="fact-ref">40% of Africa's ore exports</a>. Congolese cobalt <a href="#fact-3" id="ref-3" class="fact-ref">nearly triples in value</a> between the mine gate and local refining. Their policy conclusion is that individual countries supply too little to bargain alone, so pooled supply, shared processing and common rules matter.

The Lab's [Mineral Flows Map](/mineral-flows) adopts their four steps as its structure: deposits and production become the mining stage, trade becomes the flow layer, value-chain position becomes the stage ribbon, and policy becomes the marker layer.

## Six methodological strands

### 1. Trade-linked material flow analysis

Nansai and colleagues (2014) established the approach most relevant to a flow map: take bilateral trade from BACI across 231 economies, convert each HS commodity into its contained metal, and solve for a material balance in every country so that production, imports, exports and consumption reconcile.

Sun, Hao and co-authors applied the same logic to lithium (2017) and cobalt (2019), the latter across 249 economies for 1995 to 2015. A 2024 study in *Resources, Conservation and Recycling* extended it through four life stages (mining, refining, cathode, cell), showing roughly <a href="#fact-4" id="ref-4" class="fact-ref">69% of cobalt mined in the Democratic Republic of the Congo</a>, <a href="#fact-5" id="ref-5" class="fact-ref">80% of lithium mined in Australia and Chile</a>, <a href="#fact-6" id="ref-6" class="fact-ref">over half of refining in China</a>, and cathode and cell production in East Asia.

Dynamic variants by Zeng et al. (2022) and Dunn et al. (2021) add stocks, retirement and recycling, and show that under idealised recovery, retired batteries could meet about <a href="#fact-7" id="ref-7" class="fact-ref">60% of cobalt and 53% of lithium and nickel demand by 2040</a>.

For the map, this strand dictates the unit of account: flows must eventually be expressed in tonnes of contained metal rather than trade value, and each flow must carry a stage tag, because a tonne of spodumene concentrate and a tonne of lithium hydroxide are different things to a policymaker.

### 2. Complex network analysis of trade

A large and fast-growing literature, mostly from Chinese resource-economics groups publishing in *Resources Policy* and *Resources, Conservation and Recycling*, treats mineral trade as a weighted directed graph and reads off density, centrality, community structure and robustness.

Single-mineral networks for lithium, cobalt and nickel have given way to multi-layer designs. The lithium-ion battery supply-network study in *Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews* joins minerals, cathode and anode materials and finished cells over 1990 to 2020 and shows that single-layer networks understate systemic risk. Hao et al. (2022) couple cobalt and lithium layers to simulate how low-cobalt chemistries propagate. A 2025 study of the combined lithium, cobalt and nickel network for 2010 to 2024 finds it evolving into a tightly coupled small world.

The most useful single result for display comes from a 2026 paper in *Entropy*, which finds that <a href="#fact-8" id="ref-8" class="fact-ref">seven routes carry over 80% of lithium trade value</a>, against 81 routes for cobalt and 34 for nickel.

Two design rules follow for the Lab's map. First, show the corridors that carry the bulk of value and let the long tail sit in the data table, since plotting every link produces an unreadable hairball. Second, the number of corridors needed to reach 80% is itself a concentration indicator worth displaying beside the HHI.

### 3. Criticality and import reliance

Graedel et al. (2015) scored 62 metals on supply risk, environmental implications and vulnerability to restriction, establishing that criticality is multi-dimensional. Gulley, Nassar and Xun (2018) measured net import reliance for 42 minerals in the United States and China and found both economies heavily import-reliant on 11 of them, which frames the relationship as competition rather than simple scarcity.

Nassar et al. (2020) combined import reliance, exporter disruption potential and economic vulnerability into a supply-risk score for US manufacturing. Olivetti et al. (2017) identified by-product dependence, where cobalt follows copper and nickel output rather than its own price, as a specific bottleneck for batteries.

The stress test in the Lab's map is a stripped-down version of this logic: it removes one supplier and reports the share offline at each stage and the resulting concentration among those left.

### 4. Policy, export restrictions and resource nationalism

Kowalski and Legendre (2023) used the OECD Inventory of Export Restrictions on Industrial Raw Materials to show a <a href="#fact-9" id="ref-9" class="fact-ref">more than fivefold rise in restrictions on critical raw materials since 2009</a>, concentrated on upstream products, with export taxes and licensing the dominant instruments because WTO rules largely prohibit quantitative bans.

The follow-up paper by Andrenelli et al. (2025) examined cobalt in the DRC, lithium in Argentina and Zimbabwe and nickel in Indonesia and found the value gains concentrated in primary processing and often captured by foreign investors, with costs for trading partners and for environmental goals.

The IEA's *Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026* reports that the average share of the top refining country reached a record in 2025, that the dominant supplier accounted for nearly all supply growth in manganese, nickel and graphite, and that rare earths were the one chain where refining concentration fell, owing to new capacity in the United States and Malaysia.

The policy layer of the map therefore needs to attach each measure to a product stage, carry a date and a status, and be refreshed from the OECD inventory rather than maintained by hand.

### 5. Ownership as distinct from geography

Sun et al. (2024) built a company-level database of lithium, cobalt, nickel and platinum mining across 240 economies and found that in 2019, foreign direct investment controlled <a href="#fact-10" id="ref-10" class="fact-ref">47% of lithium, 71% of cobalt, 41% of nickel and 34% of platinum output</a>.

Supply-risk indices change materially once production is allocated to the investor's home country rather than the mine's location. This is the strongest case for a second version of the map with an ownership toggle: a map coloured by mine location overstates the control that producing countries actually exercise, which is precisely the gap the Ndubuisi et al. study and the Nordic Africa Institute's 2026 Copperbelt policy note identify.

### 6. Resilience, social and environmental risk, and regional value chains

Sprecher et al. (2015, 2017) defined material-supply resilience as the capacity to supply enough of a material, or suitable alternatives, and quantified diversity of supply, stockpiling, material efficiency and substitution as response mechanisms using the 2010 rare-earth crisis. Mancheri et al. (2019) extended the framework to Chinese quotas and consolidation.

Lebre et al. (2020) built a seven-dimension ESG matrix across 20 metals and found cobalt in high-risk contexts and copper risk rising with expansion. Owen et al. (2023) showed most transition-mineral projects sit on or near land held by Indigenous and peasant peoples.

On the African side, ECDPM (2023) mapped the continent's position in the battery chain and compared the Lobito and Dar es Salaam corridors. A 2025 Springer paper framed Lobito as a critical-raw-material axis with its own geopolitical and institutional risks. A July 2026 paper by Stonor and Kitaw with UNECA argued that the corridor should anchor copper processing rather than speed ore to the coast, estimating that <a href="#fact-11" id="ref-11" class="fact-ref">Africa mines about 17% of the world's copper but refines about 9%</a>.

These studies justify the Africa-focus lens on the map, the corridor layer, and the planned ESG overlay, and they supply the social-science framing the Lab applies before the technical one.

## What this means for the build

The literature converges on five requirements that the [Mineral Flows Map](/mineral-flows) prototype meets in part and should complete.

**Stage-disaggregated flows.** The prototype colours ore, concentrate and intermediates orange and refined metal, chemicals and components white, so the raw-export pattern the Ndubuisi study describes is visible at a glance.

**Concentration reported per stage.** The headline story in almost every chain is that refining is more concentrated than mining; the stage ribbon and the per-stage HHI carry that.

**Tonnes of contained metal.** The unit of account has to move from rounded shares and relative weights to tonnes derived from BACI HS6 data with metal-content coefficients, following Nansai et al. (2014).

**Product-specific, dated policy.** Every export restriction, local-content rule and processing incentive is attached to a stage, dated, and refreshed from the OECD inventory rather than maintained by hand.

**Ownership, ESG and recycling.** The three most requested extensions in the literature belong in version 2.

---

## Gaps this map can fill

Most network studies stop at country-level topology and publish static figures; few are interactive, and almost none put the African value-capture question at the centre. The trade-linked MFA literature is methodologically strong but rarely updated after publication.

A maintained, openly sourced tool that joins stage-specific concentration, bilateral flows, dated policy measures and the African processing gap is not something the incumbents offer. It connects directly to the Lab's [Green Industrialisation & Local Manufacturing](/expertise-manufacturing) programme.

## Verification status

Nineteen of the thirty-six entries in the underlying bibliography were seen with title, venue and link in search results during preparation. Four (Graedel 2015, Nassar 2020, Olivetti 2017 and Watari 2019) are cited from the established literature and require their DOIs clicked through. Thirteen have an author list, exact title or venue still to confirm, usually because the surfaced page was a *ScienceDirect* abstract without author names.

Each bibliography entry carries its status in a `check` field alongside the flow data. The house rule applies: no citation goes live on the public site until its link resolves.
