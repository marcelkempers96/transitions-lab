# The Transition Does Not Have a Jobs Number

*Four announcements in one week describe four completely different labour events, and only one of them is job creation. The transition's employment story depends entirely on which technology gets built, where, and who is standing nearby.*

<p class="article-meta"><span class="article-date">28 August 2026</span> · <span class="article-reading-time">5 min read</span></p>

## Four announcements, four different labour events, one meaningless statistic

Ask how many jobs the energy transition creates and you will be given a number. The number is close to meaningless, and this week supplies a clean demonstration of why.

Four announcements landed within days of each other. Each describes something happening to work. Only one of them is job creation.

| Labour event | This week's example | Effect on headcount | Durable |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transferred | Superhot geothermal drilling | Roughly neutral, moved sideways | Yes, if the technology scales |
| Relocated and thinned | 460 MW data centre load | Large temporarily, small after | No |
| Flow mistaken for stock | 100 GW solar deployment | Very large, then very small | No |
| Created, minus what it replaces | Electric scooter manufacturing | Net positive, smaller than gross | Yes |
| Displaced | Satellite verification of farm practice | Negative, quietly | Permanent |

Five rows, because displacement did not fit a story anybody announced.

---

## Transferred: the case where a worker actually moves

[Quaise Energy](https://www.quaise.energy/) has raised around US$180 million to commercialise superhot geothermal, including a strategic investment from the drilling contractor [Nabors Industries](https://www.nabors.com/), aimed at building what it describes as the first commercial superhot geothermal plant.

Geothermal is the low-carbon technology with by far the highest labour continuity from the fossil sector, and the evidence for that is unusually specific. The International Energy Agency estimates that [around two-thirds of every dollar invested in conventional geothermal operations has significant overlap with the oil and gas industry](https://www.iea.org/reports/the-future-of-geothermal-energy/overview-of-synergies-between-the-oil-and-gas-and-geothermal-industries), with the largest overlaps in project evaluation and planning, drilling and completion, surface facility construction and maintenance, and operations monitoring.

There is a second point that matters more and is almost never made in employment terms. A Congressional Research Service assessment notes that [geothermal power requires similar numbers of workers as natural gas plants, unlike solar and wind generation](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R47405). Operating labour intensity differs enormously across low-carbon technologies, and geothermal sits at the high end while the technologies attracting most deployment capital sit at the low end.

The same source supplies the necessary caution: workforce transfer potential remains [limited unless and until commercial enhanced geothermal scales up enough to employ large numbers of new workers](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R47405). This is a real pathway contingent on a technology that has not yet been proven at commercial scale. It should be argued for on those terms rather than promised.

## Relocated and thinned: the anchor load

A 460 megawatt data centre commitment is a large construction and commissioning employment event followed by a small operating one. Both facts are true and only the first gets announced.

The commissioning, high-voltage electrical, project controls and instrumentation skills that data centre construction needs are also, as it happens, the skills geothermal development needs. These two sectors are not solving each other's labour problem. They are bidding for the same people, and in the same national labour pool that oil and gas has been shedding.

We wrote separately about [what an anchor load does to the place that hosts it](/insight-the-smelter-contract). The employment version of that argument is simpler: a region evaluating a large industrial arrival should ask for the operating headcount at year five, in writing, and should treat the construction number as what it is, which is a temporary event with a known end date.

---

## Flow mistaken for stock

Indonesia's programme, [14 projects totalling around 5.3 gigawatts within a target of roughly 100 gigawatts by 2029](/insight-behind-the-border), will generate an enormous installation workforce. Panels do not mount themselves and 100 gigawatts is a great deal of mounting.

That workforce exists for the duration of the build. Operating solar employs very few people per megawatt, which is one of its virtues as a generation technology and a problem for anyone who promised jobs.

This is the most common measurement error in transition employment claims, and it is entirely avoidable. Construction employment is a flow, measured in person-years. Operating employment is a stock, measured in positions. A political commitment made on the flow number and evaluated five years later against the stock will always look like a failure, and often the programme succeeded at exactly what it was designed to do.

The domestic manufacturing ambition carries its own version of this. Building a solar cell industry and creating employment are different objectives that get announced as one. Cell manufacturing is capital-intensive and heavily automated, and it is becoming more so. A country can succeed completely at localising production and employ far fewer people than the announcement implied, without anybody having misled anyone.

## Created, minus what it replaces

[River Mobility's](https://www.rivermobility.com/) US$120 million round funds a factory capable of up to 80,000 scooters a month. That is real manufacturing employment and it should be counted as such.

It should also be counted net. An electric powertrain has far fewer components than a combustion one, so supplier employment per vehicle falls even as assembly employment rises. The gross figure and the net figure differ substantially and only the gross figure appears in the industrial policy case.

The part that is genuinely additive is the one nobody counts: service networks, battery handling, swap station operation and repair. That work is labour-intensive, geographically distributed, non-tradeable and impossible to automate away in the near term. It is also low-status, poorly measured and absent from every manufacturing employment projection we have seen.

---

## Displaced, quietly

The fifth event is the one no announcement described, and it is the automation story arriving where nobody looks for it.

[Satellite verification of farm practice](/insight-cheaper-to-verify) removes the need to send an enumerator. That is a job, held disproportionately by educated young people in rural districts, and it is being automated out of existence in exactly the programmes that describe themselves as creating rural livelihoods.

Automation in the transition is not mainly arriving in factories, where it is expected and negotiated. It is arriving in the measurement, inspection, verification and monitoring layer, where nobody has a category for it and no programme reports it.

## The constraint that adjacency statistics cannot see

Two-thirds of a geothermal investment dollar overlapping with oil and gas is a statement about skills. It is not a statement about a person.

A driller in the Permian Basin and a geothermal project in Nevada are adjacent on a skills matrix and far apart in a life containing a mortgage, a school year and a spouse's job. The just-transition literature keeps rediscovering that geographic attachment, not skill distance, is usually the binding constraint, and programmes keep being designed as though retraining were the answer. Retraining a worker who cannot move produces a qualified unemployed person in the same town.

Place attachment is also rational rather than sentimental. Selling a house in a region whose main employer has closed means selling into the worst possible market, and the equity lost is frequently larger than any retraining grant on offer.

Any credible workforce analysis therefore has to combine two things that are almost always kept apart: occupational adjacency, which is a national statistic, and travel-to-work geography, which is local and specific.

---

## What would actually be worth measuring

**Person-years by phase, never a single headline number.** Construction and operating employment separated, with the operating figure stated for year five rather than year one.

**Adjacency and distance together.** Which occupations can move to which, and whether the origin and destination are in the same labour market. Either number alone is misleading.

**The automation decisions taken between announcement and commissioning.** This is where the promised jobs go and nobody documents it, because the decisions are technical, incremental and made long after the political commitment.

**Who ends up in the jobs.** Not how many jobs, but whether the people filling them are the people who lost the previous ones. These are routinely different populations, and the distinction is the entire content of the phrase "just transition". It is also the finding most evaluations manage to avoid producing.

An honest industrial policy commitment names the phase, the occupation and the place. Most do not, because that number is much smaller than the headline and it has the disadvantage of being checkable.

The Lab works on this across [local manufacturing](/expertise-manufacturing) and [energy access](/expertise-energy), and it is the question our [field research](/field-research) is built to answer, because who ends up in the work is something you find out by asking rather than by modelling.

If you are making an employment commitment attached to a deployment programme, [the version that survives evaluation is worth constructing before the announcement](/contact).
