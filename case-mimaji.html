Field case

The Price of Not Knowing: Water Transparency in Nairobi

How open data about water quality and price becomes a socio-technical intervention in its own right, changing who can hold a water system to account. A field engagement with the MiMaji Foundation.

LocationNairobi, Kenya
SectorWater & sanitation · open environmental data · community accountability
Lab programmeWater & Sanitation Transitions

The transition in one sentence

When the people paying the most for water can finally see what they are paying for, and what is in it, the water system itself begins to change.

Why this case matters

Nairobi's water story is not primarily a story about pipes. It is a story about information. In the low-income settlements, households can pay many times more per litre than households in wealthier neighbourhoods, often for water of unknown and variable quality, bought from vendors in a market with almost no visible pricing. The technical means to test water and to map a network exist. What has been missing is transparency: a shared, public picture of who charges what, and whose water is safe.

The MiMaji Foundation works on exactly this, through community water-quality testing, WASH education in schools and community groups, and MajiMap, a public, open-data map of water quality, price, and access across the city. It is a clear instance of the Lab's central interest: a technology (open environmental data) whose effect is decided not by its accuracy but by whether it reaches, is trusted by, and is usable by the people it is meant to serve.

What a case like this asks

Open data is often assumed to empower automatically. The Lab's starting question is more sceptical, and more useful: empower whom, exactly, and to do what?

Transparency is only power if it reaches the right hands

A public map changes a market only if the people paying the most can actually see and act on it. The research question is not "is the data accurate?" but "does it reach the household in the settlement, in a form they can use, or mainly the already-connected who least need it?" That is a reach question, and it is where open-data interventions most often quietly fail.

Trust is the hinge

A price map or a quality reading changes behaviour only if people believe it. In a market thick with rumour and vendor interest, the credibility of the source decides whether the information travels. Who is seen to stand behind the data matters as much as the data.

The behaviour that follows is the real outcome

The point of transparency is not the map; it is what changes because of it, whether a household switches source, whether a vendor adjusts a price under visible comparison, whether a community body gains a lever it did not have. Measuring that behaviour, read across reach, depth, and experience, is the actual evaluation.

What the Lab brings to a case like this

The Lab reads MajiMap not as a data product to be assessed for accuracy, but as a socio-technical intervention to be evaluated for effect. It pairs Delft-rooted water understanding with primary fieldwork in the settlements, asking who reaches the information, who trusts it, and what changes when the price of not knowing finally has a number attached.

Through the BRW framework, MajiMap reads as a weaken strategy against a soft but powerful barrier: the information asymmetry that lets an opaque water market persist. It does not build a parallel water system (bypass) or redirect the utility (repurpose); it erodes the informational foundation on which the inequity rests. That reading tells the Lab exactly where to look for success or failure: in whether the transparency actually reaches and is trusted by those on the wrong side of the asymmetry.

The wider reading

Water transparency in Nairobi is a specific case of a general pattern the Lab studies everywhere: technologies whose effect is informational and distributional rather than physical. The same logic runs through open data in energy, health, and agriculture. In each, the question is not whether the information is correct, but whether it lands where the power imbalance is, and shifts it.
