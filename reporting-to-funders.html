Client question 03

Reporting to Funders

Turning a project's evidence into a defensible account of what changed, for whom, and through what pathway. Set up at the start, run throughout, closed out with proof.

Most projects can tell a funder what they did. Far fewer can show what changed because of it, for whom, and whether it will last, or turn that evidence into something the audience can act on. This is the discipline the Lab runs as an independent partner, on privately commissioned work and on European projects with a Grant Agreement to satisfy alike.

Reporting to Funders is one connected system, not three afterthoughts. The three parts, and the one where the EU rules add specifics, are set out below.

Monitoring, Evaluation & Dissemination

Monitoring

Continuous tracking of what a project is doing and reaching while it is still running. It answers "are we on course?" in time to change course. Monitoring without evaluation produces activity counts that no one learns from.

Evaluation

Periodic, structured judgement of whether the project is working, and why. It answers "is this the right thing, is it working, and will it last?" Evaluation without monitoring arrives too late, with no baseline to judge against.

Dissemination

The deliberate work of getting results to the audiences who can use them: other researchers, users, policymakers, the public, in a form they can act on. Evidence that stays in a final report has failed at the last step.

The three are one loop. Monitoring feeds evaluation; evaluation sharpens what is worth disseminating; dissemination and its uptake become the next thing to monitor. Designed together from day one, they make a project legible to itself and credible to everyone else.

Built on established evaluation science

MED is not improvised. It rests on decades of evaluation methodology, and the Lab works explicitly within these frameworks so that findings are defensible and comparable.

Theory of change and the results chain. Every design begins by making the project's causal logic explicit: the chain from inputs and activities to outputs, outcomes, and impact. This is the backbone of results-based management and the logical framework tradition (W.K. Kellogg Foundation, 2004; UNDP, 2009). Naming the chain in advance is what lets you test it later, rather than asserting success after the fact.

The OECD-DAC evaluation criteria. The Lab judges interventions against the six internationally standard criteria: relevance, coherence, effectiveness, efficiency, impact, and sustainability. These give donors and evaluation teams a shared language for asking not just "did it work?" but "was it the right thing to do, and will the benefits last?"

Reach, depth, experience. The Lab reads impact across three dimensions from its own measurement method, so evaluation stays attentive to distribution and equity, which aggregate figures routinely hide. See Measuring Change for the frame.

Baselines and mixed methods. Impact is a change, and a change needs a before. Every design captures a baseline with the same instrument that will be used again later, and combines quantitative reach with qualitative depth.

For European projects

For projects funded by the European Union Independent measurement designed to satisfy funder review, survive audit, and tell the project team something true. See the European Impact Tracking service →

Europe funds innovation at a scale unmatched anywhere, across Horizon Europe, the European Social Fund Plus, the LIFE programme, the Just Transition Fund, the European Innovation Council, Interreg, Digital Europe, the EIT KICs, and the New European Bauhaus. The funding is excellent. The evidence of what it actually changed, for whom, too often is not.

Horizon Europe treats communication, dissemination, and exploitation as three distinct required activities under Article 17 of its Model Grant Agreement, and expects a Plan for Dissemination and Exploitation including Communication activities (PDEC), typically due by month 6. The most common evaluator criticism is conflating the three into one vague block; they must be structured separately, each with its own audience, purpose, and channel.

The Lab acts as the independent measurement partner:

  • Set up at the start. A theory of change, an indicator framework, a baseline, and a clean separation of communication, dissemination, and exploitation, designed to survive review.
  • Run throughout. Continuous monitoring against indicators, midline reads that let the consortium steer while there is still time, and an evidence trail that maps to periodic reporting.
  • Close with proof. An endline evaluation against the DAC criteria and the project's own targets, with dissemination that genuinely reaches the intended audiences and the documentation to show it.

Common failure modes on European projects

What derails a Grant Agreement
  • The baseline is missing. Measurement designed near the end, so the "before" is reconstructed from memory. Fix with an impact-tracking template at project design.
  • Methods are inconsistent and incomparable. Every partner measures differently. The Better Regulation guidelines call for comparability from day one.
  • Output is mistaken for impact. Participants reached, workshops delivered. These are activities, not outcomes. The OECD DAC criteria separate output from outcome from impact.
  • The people most affected are rarely heard systematically. Our in-depth interview guide documents the reach principle.
  • Self-assessment strains credibility. Independent measurement exists precisely to counter it.
  • Evidence arrives too late to use. When measurement is an end-of-project exercise, findings land after every meaningful decision has already been made.

What you receive

Every engagement ends in something the reporting audience can act on.

  • Clean, well-documented data, in the format that fits your team.
  • A written analysis that states plainly what the evidence shows and what it cannot.
  • The instruments themselves (surveys, interview guides, indicator sets) so the study can be repeated.
  • Dissemination assets that carry the findings to the audiences who can use them.
  • A defensible reporting spine mapped to the DAC criteria and the project's own targets.

Turnaround is measured in weeks, not seasons.
