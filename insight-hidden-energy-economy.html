The Hidden Energy Economy: What Nairobi's Unofficial Battery Chargers Say About the Swap Network

A hidden battery charger in Nairobi's Industrial Area shows how boda boda riders route around thin swap networks, and what operators and regulators can learn from it.

Share

1 November 2026 · 6 min read

A rumour on Suguta Road

I first heard about the place on the back of a motorcycle, going home from a day of interviews this autumn. The rider, on a Roam bike, pulled into one of the official charging points along Suguta Road, took one look at the queue and the bikes lined up against the wall, and told me almost in passing that if you needed a battery quickly there was another place, somewhere in Industrial Area, that nobody talked about. He didn't know exactly where. Only that riders in a hurry went there, and that it was faster than the hub.

For the next three days I asked everyone I could. Mechanics at the roadside spots, attendants at the official points, riders at the hubs. Each of them either shrugged, said they'd heard of such places without knowing one, or changed the subject with the kind of politeness that tells you the question has been understood perfectly well. In a city where a fundi can direct you to a specific clutch plate in a specific shop three streets away, the silence was itself a finding. Informal businesses in Nairobi are usually advertised loudly. This one was being protected.

The person who broke the silence was a rider I had interviewed five months earlier. He recognised my face at the Roam hub on Lusaka Road, remembered our conversation and, after some hesitation, decided he would take me there himself.

Seven minutes into Industrial Area

The ride took about seven minutes from Lusaka Road, off the tarmac and into the grid of gravel lanes behind the front-facing buildings of Industrial Area, where the city's workshops, fabricators and scrap dealers occupy the plots the main roads hide. It was dry, so the lanes were dust and loose stone rather than mud, and a couple of dogs wandered between parked cars with the unhurried confidence of animals that live there. My rider stopped outside a high gate set into a fence behind one of the larger buildings, kept the engine running and made it clear that this was as far as he intended to go, and that he would rather I did not go further either.

I said I wanted to see it. He shrugged and told me to go ahead. The gate opened as I approached, for reasons I never established, and I walked straight in holding a sheet of paper, wearing the formal clothes I'd put on for an interview that morning. In hindsight I looked like a man on official business.

Inside felt less like a workshop than a small industrial theme park. Welders cutting steel under the open sky. Stainless-steel tanks and frames stacked against the walls. A fabricator's banner on the building next door. Sacks of scrap waiting to be sorted. The smell of hot metal and dust over all of it.

In the middle of this, built into a corrugated-iron structure painted blue at the base and silver above, was the thing I had come for. A solar panel bolted to the wall above the entrance. A CCTV camera watching the yard from a bracket beside it. A handwritten sheet taped to the frame with a phone number. Below them, a tall white cabinet divided into fifteen locker compartments, each with its own door, so similar to the cabinets at official hubs that for a moment I wondered whether I had misunderstood the rumour. Parked outside was an electric boda boda with a green 100% Electric sticker on its tank, battery bay open, waiting.

The man who tried to do it officially

The owner, whom I will not name, since the business he runs sits in a regulatory grey zone, came out to meet me with the guarded courtesy of someone who hadn't expected a visitor and was still deciding what kind of visitor I was. Once it was clear I was a researcher rather than an inspector, he talked for longer than I had any right to expect. What he described was not a scheme to cheat anyone. It was a business that had started out trying to be legitimate.

He had first approached the charging business through the official route, hoping to become one of the partner sites operators were recruiting as they expanded. He invested his own money in the cabinet, the wiring, the solar panel on the strength of that plan. Then the company's direction changed. The network grew through its own hubs and larger stations rather than through small independent partners like him. He was left with equipment bought for a purpose that no longer existed, a yard in a part of the city where hundreds of riders pass every day, and, from conversations with those riders, a clear view of how much of their working day disappeared at the official hubs.

So he built what he called his own thing. Riders who own their batteries, or who are caught short in the morning rush, bring them to him and leave them in a locker to charge while they wait or go off on a short trip. They pay him a fee set to be fair to riders who are already paying a daily loan on their bikes. Most customers come by word of mouth, which is how he wants it, and most return because the wait at his cabinet is shorter than at the hubs nearby. He spoke about them less as customers than as people he was helping to stay on the road, and he was visibly proud that a rider who might otherwise lose a morning could be back carrying passengers within an hour.

He was also clear about the limits. He knew he didn't hold a licence for a charging station. He knew the electricity he used wasn't billed under the e-mobility tariff that licensed operators receive. He knew that a business without papers in Industrial Area is a business anyone with a badge, or a claim to one, can visit whenever they like. That is why the gate stays closed, why the riders who know about it don't tell researchers with notebooks, and why the rider who brought me had stopped outside.

Two men in suits

I found out how real that last point was when I was leaving. Walking back towards the gate, two men came in: both in formal clothes like mine, both with scars across their faces, walking with the unhurried purpose of people who knew exactly where they were going. My first thought was that they were electricians or inspectors come to check a meter, and I was about to ask them about the installation when my rider appeared at the gate, caught my eye, and told me in a low voice that it was time to go.

On the ride back he explained what he thought I had seen. Plain-clothes officers, he said, make regular rounds of unlicensed businesses in areas like this one, looking for a missing permit or an irregular connection. The visit usually ends with a payment rather than a closure. I can't know who those two men were and I would not want to accuse anyone on the strength of a glance at a gate. But the rider's reading fitted everything the owner had told me about the cost of operating without papers. A business the regulator cannot see is still visible to people who profit from its invisibility. That cost is passed, eventually, to the riders who pay for each charge.

What a workaround is telling us

It would be easy to read the site as a problem to shut down. Some risks are real. Lithium cells charged on equipment without type approval, outside the battery management systems that operators use to monitor cell temperature and health, can fail in ways that are dangerous in a corrugated-iron shed next to a welding yard. Kenya's Electric Vehicle Charging and Battery Swapping Infrastructure Guidelines, in force since September 2023, require charging equipment to be KEBS-approved and swapping batteries to be BMS-enabled, and both requirements exist for good reasons (Oraro & Company Advocates, 2023; Obura, 2023).

The more useful reading is to treat the site as a measurement. The scarce resource for electric boda boda riders is time at the hub, where a battery out of stock can turn a ten-second swap into an afternoon on a bench. A hidden cabinet in Industrial Area, protected by riders who will not even tell a familiar researcher where it is, is what that scarcity looks like when someone decides to sell a solution to it. Riders are paying a stranger behind a locked gate, at some risk to their batteries and their warranties, because the hour they save is worth more to them than the safety and convenience the official network promises but cannot always deliver. Simone (2004) described how residents of African cities become infrastructure for one another, assembling services from improvised connections where formal systems are thin. Nairobi's electric transition is already producing its own version of that, nine months after a national policy designed to formalise it.

The contradictions are visible in the rules themselves. The 2023 guidelines require public charging and swapping to be accessible to all electric vehicles regardless of make (Kenyans.co.ke, 2023), yet in practice each operator runs its own batteries on its own cabinets, so a rider whose operator's hub is empty cannot walk across the road to a competitor. The National Electric Mobility Policy launched in February 2026 commits to compatible charging and standardised battery swapping (Down To Earth, 2026), but that commitment has not yet reached the bench at the hub. Licensing is built for operators with legal teams: each station in a chain requires its own retail supply licence (CrossBoundary Energy, 2024), a threshold a man with one cabinet and a solar panel is unlikely to cross. Even the welcome removal of the 15,000 kWh monthly ceiling on the e-mobility tariff in September 2026 (Techweez, 2026) helps the large networks whose consumption is growing fastest, while doing nothing for the small sites that sit outside the tariff altogether.

The scale of the formal networks makes the gap easier to see, not harder. Spiro, whose Swap and Go stations now stand on forecourts across the city, reported more than 400 swap stations in Kenya by early 2026 (Kuhudzai, 2026); an estimated 90% of the country's charging infrastructure is concentrated in Nairobi (TheCityFix, 2026). If riders in the best-served city in the country are still seeking out a locked yard in Industrial Area, the shortage is not about the number of stations on a map. It is about whether a charged battery is waiting at the right place at seven in the morning.

From hidden cabinet to licensed agent

Kenya has solved a version of this problem before. M-Pesa did not reach every village by building company branches but by licensing tens of thousands of small shopkeepers as agents, each operating under the network's rules and oversight while keeping their own premises and customers. Battery charging could follow a similar path. The owner I met is in many ways the agent that model would need: he has the site, the equipment, the riders' trust, the demonstrated willingness to do it properly if a proper route were open to him.

Three changes would move sites like his from behind the gate into the network.

A light licence tier for small charging and swapping points. Simplified electrical inspection, a single registration covering an operator's partner sites, and access to the e-mobility tariff, so the cost of becoming legal is lower than the cost of paying for invisibility.

Operator partner programmes revived and expanded. Certified cabinets connected to the operator's battery management system, so a battery charged in a yard in Industrial Area is monitored as closely as one at a hub, and the operator carries the liability rather than a stranger.

Make the official network report what riders already know. Publish battery availability and waiting times by hub and by hour, so regulators and investors see where the informal economy is filling gaps before it fills them with uninspected equipment.

None of this requires anyone to pretend that unlicensed charging is safe or that it should simply be tolerated. It requires taking seriously the information that a workaround carries: that riders have priced their time, found the official network wanting at the moments that matter most, and are willing to pay someone else to close the gap. The owner behind the gate saw that gap before most of the policy documents did. The question for Kenya's e-mobility transition is whether people like him will be brought inside the network as agents, or left outside it paying for the privilege of being overlooked.
