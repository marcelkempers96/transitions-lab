The EU and Africa: Opportunity, Compatibility, and the Evidence In Between

Europe is committing hundreds of billions to Africa, and Africa is becoming the largest growth market of the century. Where do the two genuinely fit, and what stands between the investment and the impact it promises?

July 2026 · 5 min read

By Marcel Kempers

Two facts are reshaping the relationship between Europe and Africa. Europe needs new markets, secure supply chains, and clean-energy partners. Africa needs investment, industrialisation, and jobs at a scale no other region will match. On paper, the interests align almost perfectly. The question is whether the alignment survives contact with reality, and that is a question about evidence.

Africa is the growth market of the century

The demographic arithmetic is unlike anywhere else. Africa's population is projected to nearly double from 1.5 to 2.5 billion between 2025 and 2050, the highest regional growth rate in the world, accounting for more than half of global population growth through 2050. By mid-century, hundreds of millions of Africans are expected to join the middle class, with consumer and business spending projected to reach $16.1 trillion.

Crucially for Europe, this is a market with room to build, not just to sell into. As the world's least-industrialised continent, contributing less than 2% of global manufacturing, Africa offers the greatest opportunities for productivity gains and per-capita income growth. The African Continental Free Trade Area is the mechanism meant to unlock it: a single market across 54 countries in which manufactured goods scale first through AfCFTA-enabled regional demand, then expand into global markets.

A €400 billion bet, and what it targets

Europe has moved. Through the Global Gateway, the EU has pledged large-scale investment framed explicitly around mutual benefit - a €400 billion package for Africa in particular. As von der Leyen put it, Africa is a rising continent creating opportunity in traditional sectors like agriculture but also in new industries from clean energy to critical minerals and AI, and Europe wants to be a partner because the interests are shared.

The investment concentrates on a few strategic areas: critical raw materials with a focus on local beneficiation, pharmaceutical value chains, transport corridors and digital connectivity. The strategic logic is not hidden. The initiative aims to secure critical minerals, raw materials and clean energy along diversified and resilient supply chains, with flagship corridors like Lobito, connecting Angola, the DRC, and Zambia, designed to unlock mineral trade and support AfCFTA logistics integration.

Against Africa's infrastructure financing gap of nearly $100 billion annually, the Global Gateway pledge, if mobilised through public-private partnerships, could significantly narrow that divide.

Shared interest is not the same as shared benefit

Here is where honest analysis has to slow down. The interests align, but alignment on paper has a long history of failing on the ground. The candid assessment, even from observers sympathetic to the initiative, is that European funding mechanisms have historically been slow, fragmented, and burdened by bureaucracy, and past initiatives often struggled to translate ambition into on-the-ground impact.

There is also a genuine question of terms. The difference between extraction and partnership turns on a single principle that runs through the EU's own framing: local beneficiation and value addition, rather than just raw material exports. Whether critical-minerals investment builds African processing capacity and jobs, or simply ships raw ore north more efficiently, is not settled by the size of the cheque. It is settled by how the projects are actually designed and who captures their value, exactly the distributional question that only field evidence can answer.

The prize, if it is done well, is real: turning Africa's role not just into a resource provider but a value-adding player in the global green economy.

The missing evidence layer in EU-Africa investment

Between a €400 billion pledge and a genuinely transformed livelihood sits a long chain of assumptions. Does the corridor create the local jobs it promised? Does the mini-grid reach the households, or only the mine? Does local beneficiation actually happen, or is it a line in a press release? These are not questions European capitals or African governments can answer from a distance, and they are not questions a project can credibly answer about itself.

This is the Lab's territory. Every one of the Global Gateway's priority sectors, clean energy, critical minerals and manufacturing, transport, digital, is a socio-technical transition the Lab studies, and each carries the Lab's characteristic question: not whether the investment was made, but what it changed, for whom, and who was left out.

The Lab's role in the EU-Africa relationship is the independent evidence layer. For European funders and consortia, that means credible, before-and-after measurement of whether flagship investments deliver the local value they promise, the kind of proof that protects a programme's legitimacy and sharpens its next phase. For the partnership as a whole, it means holding the "mutual benefit" claim to account with real field evidence, which is the only thing that can distinguish genuine partnership from extraction with better branding. The Lab's rootedness on both continents, based in Delft with sustained field presence across Africa, is precisely the position from which that evidence can be gathered honestly.

Europe and Africa are more compatible than at any point in memory. Whether that compatibility becomes shared prosperity depends on execution, and execution is impossible to steer without honest evidence of what is actually happening on the ground.
