Where transition minerals go: the evidence base

A structured synthesis of thirty-six studies on how the transition mineral literature measures cross-border flows, concentration and value capture. Prepared as the reference paper behind the Lab's Mineral Flows Map and its companion article Where transition minerals actually go.

Open the interactive Mineral Flows Map →

New to the map? Read the sixty-second guide. For every source behind every figure, see the data attribution page.

🔗 Open-access paper: transitionslab.org/mineral-flows-research · interactive companion · headline article

  • Much of the existing literature on critical-mineral supply focuses on country-level production shares and the Chinese share of refining. This paper instead examines the cross-border architecture of fifteen transition-mineral chains, from mine to refinery to cathode / magnet / anode, as a single interactive map backed by 36 studies.
  • We draw on USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025 for mining, reserves and headline production, the IEA Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026 for refining and downstream capacity, and 36 peer-reviewed and institutional studies for the stage-level flows. 298 bilateral corridors across 60 countries; 250 sourced country shares; every figure carries a confidence marker.
  • Our seven findings highlight a concern that goes beyond the pace of refining concentration itself: the gap between where minerals sit, where they move and where the value is captured.
  • Africa mines roughly 17% of the world's copper but refines about 9%. Congolese cobalt nearly triples in value between the mine gate and local refining. 48 African countries hold reserves of at least one transition mineral but around a dozen appear as current exporters.
  • Export restrictions on critical minerals have risen more than fivefold since 2009, all of it upstream on ore and concentrate. Foreign direct investment controls 47% of world lithium, 71% of cobalt, 41% of nickel and 34% of platinum; a map by mine location overstates the control producing countries actually exercise.
  • If recycling runs at idealised recovery, retired batteries could meet about 60% of cobalt and 53% of lithium and nickel demand by 2040. The ceiling exists; the conditions do not hold today.
  • Broader than concentration numbers, a mass-balanced bilateral map, grouped by role in an EV and anchored to public primary sources, is the diagnostic the sector lacks. Policies that facilitate processing diffusion, Indigenous and peasant-land governance, and end-of-life capture will matter more than any single export restriction. International institutions such as the UN Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD), the World Bank Group and the International Energy Agency can play an important role in supporting such cooperation and helping countries address the risks of transition-mineral concentration and value loss.

Independent synthesis by Transitions Lab, under open peer-review via the bibliography links. Data: transitionslab.org/mineral-flows-attribution.

Scope: what counts as a transition mineral here

The paper covers the fifteen chains on the companion Mineral Flows Map, grouped by the job they do in an electric vehicle. Battery chains are lithium, nickel, cobalt, manganese, graphite, phosphate and fluorspar; they feed the cathode, anode, electrolyte and binder of a lithium-ion pack. Motor and wiring chains are rare earths, boron and copper; they feed the permanent magnets in the drive motor and the current path to and from them. Electronics and charging chains are silicon metal, gallium and silver; they feed the power chips, inverter devices, charger electronics and solar cell interconnect. A fourth group, beyond the EV, covers platinum group metals and germanium: catalytic converters and fuel cells for one, fibre optics and infrared sensors for the other. The scope is "transition minerals," not "EV minerals only," because the transition changes demand for both ends of that pair; the EV is where new demand concentrates fastest, and the catalyst chains are where legacy demand falls.

Grouping is also how the map's chip row is organised: the four group chips above the mineral chips let a reader see one job's chains at a time without renaming the tool. The findings below are the same across the four groups, which is itself a finding.

Starting point

This paper takes its cue from Ndubuisi, Avenyo, Owusu and Kassa's World Bank study, republished on 29 September 2026 by The Conversation and the South Africa Trade Desk. Their method runs in four steps.

  1. Deposits. They identify which transition minerals each African country holds.
  2. Trade. They follow the CEPII BACI trade data to see who exports what to whom.
  3. Value-chain position. They place each country on the green-technology value chain according to whether it exports ore, processed material or components.
  4. Policy. They review the national and regional policy attached to each stage.

They find forty-eight African countries hold reserves of at least one transition mineral, and that most of what does leave the continent leaves raw or lightly processed, with Asia and China in particular taking over 40% of Africa's ore exports. The distinction between holding a reserve and exporting at scale matters for the map: today only around a dozen African countries appear on it as source hubs, because the rest are pre-export. Congolese cobalt nearly triples in value between the mine gate and local refining. Their policy conclusion is that individual countries supply too little to bargain alone, so pooled supply, shared processing and common rules matter.

The Lab's Mineral Flows Map adopts their four steps as its structure: deposits and production become the mining stage, trade becomes the flow layer, value-chain position becomes the stage ribbon, and policy becomes the marker layer.

What an electric vehicle actually contains

The passenger EV in the diagram carries at least eight mineral groups, each with its own supply chain, its own concentration pattern, and its own policy layer. The rest of this paper works through the six methodological strands of the literature that measure how those flows behave.

Six methodological strands

1. Trade-linked material flow analysis

Nansai and colleagues (2014) established the approach most relevant to a flow map: take bilateral trade from BACI across 231 economies, convert each HS commodity into its contained metal, and solve for a material balance in every country so that production, imports, exports and consumption reconcile.

Sun, Hao and co-authors applied the same logic to lithium (2017) and cobalt (2019), the latter across 249 economies for 1995 to 2015. A 2024 study in Resources, Conservation and Recycling extended it through four life stages (mining, refining, cathode, cell), showing roughly 69% of cobalt mined in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, 80% of lithium mined in Australia and Chile, over half of refining in China, and cathode and cell production in East Asia.

Dynamic variants by Zeng et al. (2022) and Dunn et al. (2021) add stocks, retirement and recycling, and show that under idealised recovery, retired batteries could meet about 60% of cobalt and 53% of lithium and nickel demand by 2040.

For the map, this strand dictates the unit of account: flows must eventually be expressed in tonnes of contained metal rather than trade value, and each flow must carry a stage tag, because a tonne of spodumene concentrate and a tonne of lithium hydroxide are different things to a policymaker.

2. Complex network analysis of trade

A large and fast-growing literature, mostly from Chinese resource-economics groups publishing in Resources Policy and Resources, Conservation and Recycling, treats mineral trade as a weighted directed graph and reads off density, centrality, community structure and robustness.

Single-mineral networks for lithium, cobalt and nickel have given way to multi-layer designs. The lithium-ion battery supply-network study in Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews joins minerals, cathode and anode materials and finished cells over 1990 to 2020 and shows that single-layer networks understate systemic risk. Hao et al. (2022) couple cobalt and lithium layers to simulate how low-cobalt chemistries propagate. A 2025 study of the combined lithium, cobalt and nickel network for 2010 to 2024 finds it evolving into a tightly coupled small world.

The most useful single result for display comes from a 2026 paper in Entropy, which finds that seven routes carry over 80% of lithium trade value, against 81 routes for cobalt and 34 for nickel.

Two design rules follow for the Lab's map. First, show the corridors that carry the bulk of value and let the long tail sit in the data table, since plotting every link produces an unreadable hairball. Second, the number of corridors needed to reach 80% is itself a concentration indicator worth displaying beside the HHI.

3. Criticality and import reliance

Graedel et al. (2015) scored 62 metals on supply risk, environmental implications and vulnerability to restriction, establishing that criticality is multi-dimensional. Gulley, Nassar and Xun (2018) measured net import reliance for 42 minerals in the United States and China and found both economies heavily import-reliant on 11 of them, which frames the relationship as competition rather than simple scarcity.

Nassar et al. (2020) combined import reliance, exporter disruption potential and economic vulnerability into a supply-risk score for US manufacturing. Olivetti et al. (2017) identified by-product dependence, where cobalt follows copper and nickel output rather than its own price, as a specific bottleneck for batteries.

The stress test in the Lab's map is a stripped-down version of this logic: it removes one supplier and reports the share offline at each stage and the resulting concentration among those left.

4. Policy, export restrictions and resource nationalism

Kowalski and Legendre (2023) used the OECD Inventory of Export Restrictions on Industrial Raw Materials to show a more than fivefold rise in restrictions on critical raw materials since 2009, concentrated on upstream products, with export taxes and licensing the dominant instruments because WTO rules largely prohibit quantitative bans.

The follow-up paper by Andrenelli et al. (2025) examined cobalt in the DRC, lithium in Argentina and Zimbabwe and nickel in Indonesia and found the value gains concentrated in primary processing and often captured by foreign investors, with costs for trading partners and for environmental goals.

The IEA's Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026 reports that the average share of the top refining country reached a record in 2025, that the dominant supplier accounted for nearly all supply growth in manganese, nickel and graphite, and that rare earths were the one chain where refining concentration fell, owing to new capacity in the United States and Malaysia.

The policy layer of the map therefore needs to attach each measure to a product stage, carry a date and a status, and be refreshed from the OECD inventory rather than maintained by hand.

5. Ownership as distinct from geography

Sun et al. (2024) built a company-level database of lithium, cobalt, nickel and platinum mining across 240 economies and found that in 2019, foreign direct investment controlled 47% of lithium, 71% of cobalt, 41% of nickel and 34% of platinum output.

Supply-risk indices change materially once production is allocated to the investor's home country rather than the mine's location. This is the strongest case for a second version of the map with an ownership toggle: a map coloured by mine location overstates the control that producing countries actually exercise, which is precisely the gap the Ndubuisi et al. study and the Nordic Africa Institute's 2026 Copperbelt policy note identify.

6. Resilience, social and environmental risk, and regional value chains

Sprecher et al. (2015, 2017) defined material-supply resilience as the capacity to supply enough of a material, or suitable alternatives, and quantified diversity of supply, stockpiling, material efficiency and substitution as response mechanisms using the 2010 rare-earth crisis. Mancheri et al. (2019) extended the framework to Chinese quotas and consolidation.

Lebre et al. (2020) built a seven-dimension ESG matrix across 20 metals and found cobalt in high-risk contexts and copper risk rising with expansion. Owen et al. (2023) showed most transition-mineral projects sit on or near land held by Indigenous and peasant peoples.

On the African side, ECDPM (2023) mapped the continent's position in the battery chain and compared the Lobito and Dar es Salaam corridors. A 2025 Springer paper framed Lobito as a critical-raw-material axis with its own geopolitical and institutional risks. A July 2026 paper by Stonor and Kitaw with UNECA argued that the corridor should anchor copper processing rather than speed ore to the coast, estimating that Africa mines about 17% of the world's copper but refines about 9%.

These studies justify the Africa-focus lens on the map, the corridor layer, and the planned ESG overlay, and they supply the social-science framing the Lab applies before the technical one.

What this means for the build

The literature converges on five requirements that the Mineral Flows Map prototype meets in part and should complete.

Stage-disaggregated flows. The prototype colours ore, concentrate and intermediates orange and refined metal, chemicals and components white, so the raw-export pattern the Ndubuisi study describes is visible at a glance.

Concentration reported per stage. The headline story in almost every chain is that refining is more concentrated than mining; the stage ribbon and the per-stage HHI carry that.

Tonnes of contained metal. The unit of account has to move from rounded shares and relative weights to tonnes derived from BACI HS6 data with metal-content coefficients, following Nansai et al. (2014).

Product-specific, dated policy. Every export restriction, local-content rule and processing incentive is attached to a stage, dated, and refreshed from the OECD inventory rather than maintained by hand.

Ownership, ESG and recycling. The three most requested extensions in the literature belong in version 2.

Method: tonnes-per-annum figures on the map

The Overview panel of the map surfaces an absolute tonnes-per-year figure alongside each country share ("China 68% ≈ 122 kt Li/y"). This section names exactly how each figure is derived, the world total it rests on, and the epistemic status the reader should give it.

Derivation. For every (mineral, stage) pair, the map stores a world_total field on the underlying JSON node. A country's absolute output is then computed at render time as country_share_percent × world_total / 100. Nothing more sophisticated is layered on top: the tonnes are a linear projection of the shipped share against the shipped world headline. Where a stage does not have a defensible world total (all downstream cathode-active-material stages, most refined semi-finished stages that are usually reported by capacity rather than output), the map shows the share only.

World totals: sources and confidence. Every world total on the map is a rounded headline value drawn from one of three public series. USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025 is the primary source for every mining stage and for refined copper, refined silver, silicon metal, boron oxide equivalent, fluorspar, gallium and germanium. IEA Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026 is the primary source for battery-relevant refining stages (lithium chemicals, refined nickel by class, refined cobalt, rare-earth separation, battery-grade manganese sulphate, LFP-grade purified phosphoric acid, spherical anode-grade graphite). Industry-association market reports cover four stages the two above do not (Cobalt Institute 2024 for refined cobalt cross-check; Johnson Matthey PGM Market Report 2024 for refined platinum + palladium; World Silver Survey 2024 for refined silver; International Copper Study Group World Copper Factbook 2024 for refined copper). Two frontier stages (silicon carbide power devices; NdFeB magnets) draw on a proprietary tracker (Yole Development; Adamas Intelligence) and are marked Low confidence — verify before citing.

Units and containment. Every world total is expressed in the same content unit named on the stage node (t Li content, kt Cu content, kt CaF2, t Ga, etc.). Where a mineral is normally reported gross-of-mineral (phosphate rock, borate ore, ore-grade rare-earth concentrate), the map stores the P2O5 / B2O3 / REO content equivalent, following the USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries convention. This matters when comparing to industry reports that quote the gross figure: 220 Mt of phosphate rock at ~30% P2O5 gives the ~65 Mt P2O5 content figure the map uses.

Rounding rules. All world totals on the map are rounded to two significant figures. Country tonnes derived from them are then rounded again by the fmtTonnes function at render time (t/y under 1 kt, kt/y to one decimal, Mt/y to one decimal). Two-significant-figure rounding is chosen so that the reader is never given false precision on a figure that itself carries a ±10-15% error band year-on-year.

Uncertainty. What this method cannot claim. The map's tonnes are shape-of-the-chain figures, not audited inventories. Three known limits.

  1. Share × total is a projection, not a measurement. USGS shares and USGS world totals for the same commodity are internally consistent for a given year; IEA capacity shares and IEA output totals likewise. Where the map uses a USGS share against an IEA total (rare, but done for chains where IEA breaks refining stages out further than USGS does), the two carry different reporting boundaries. The BACI trade-refresh pipeline (below) resolves this.
  1. Refining ≠ output. For several chains (nickel, rare earths, LFP-grade acid) the industry data available is installed refining capacity, not annual metal output. The map notes this on each affected stage and treats the two as approximately interchangeable, which they are within ±20% at annual resolution.
  1. Year-on-year drift. Every headline is a 2024 or 2025 figure. Lithium in particular has moved ±30% year-on-year at the world-total level between 2022 and 2024 because of Chinese lepidolite and Zimbabwean spodumene ramps; the map refreshes against USGS MCS annually within four weeks of publication (typically late January).

Corridor coverage, 1 October 2026 expansion. The 1.1 dataset shipped 95 corridors, a demonstration seed, not a supply map. A stress test asking "what if China goes offline for copper?" returned three corridors, the ones that happened to be in the seed. The 1.3 dataset ships 298 corridors across 60 countries, each at the "known to occur in meaningful volume" bar from the sources listed above. Counts per mineral range from 10 (germanium) to 39 (copper), reflecting the actual sprawl of each chain, not the convenience of the seed. A China cut for copper now returns 13 corridors (Chile, Peru, Australia, Indonesia, Mongolia, DR Congo, Zambia, Kazakhstan, Russia, Brazil, Argentina, Philippines and the inbound Chinese exports to Switzerland, Germany, Korea and Japan). Confidence markers are honest: a corridor in a USGS Mineral Industry Survey or a Johnson Matthey PGM report is high; one carried by an industry-association market report is medium; one that depends on company disclosures the Lab has not been able to cross-check is low. Each confidence marker shows next to its corridor in the Stress-test tab. Weights stay on the 1-5 relative scale until the BACI refresh replaces them with contained-metal tonnages.

Method: reserves and the mining gap

The 1.3 dataset adds a reserves field to twelve of the fifteen minerals (silicon, gallium and germanium are omitted; USGS does not publish standalone reserves for them because silicon is sand-grade quartz and gallium / germanium are co-products of bauxite and zinc refining). Reserves are the economically recoverable share of identified resources at the prevailing price, as the US Geological Survey assesses it each January in its Mineral Commodity Summaries.

Every reserves share on the map is read from the USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025 Reserves column, rounded to the nearest percentage point, and normalised to the sum of listed countries so that an OTHER bucket carries the fragmented tail. World totals are quoted in the unit USGS uses for each mineral: kilotonnes of lithium content for lithium, million tonnes of P2O5 content for phosphate rock, tonnes of combined platinum-plus-palladium for PGM, and so on. The attribution page restates the unit and the source line for every entry.

The feature the Reserves tab adds is the gap: a country's share of world reserves (what the ground holds) minus its share of world mining today (what the smelters currently move). A positive gap is the reader's cue to look for latent potential; a negative gap says the country is mining faster than its share of reserves can sustain. The gap is a share-space comparison, not a quantity, so interpreting it needs the world totals both sides reference. A country holding 3% of world lithium reserves but mining 0.5% of world lithium today has a +2.5-point gap; that reads as under-exploited only against the broader context of lithium being the second most actively invested transition-minerals space of the last decade.

The feature is particularly diagnostic for Africa. Ndubuisi, Avenyo, Owusu and Kassa report that 48 African countries hold reserves of at least one transition mineral; the map's Mining stage lists around a dozen as current exporters. The gap is the formal expression of that difference. The Reserves tab lists any African country with a reserves share more than 2 points above its mining share as having "latent potential"; readers can click a mineral chip to see the full table for every country.

Limits. First, reserves are an economic category, not a geological one: a rise in price moves resources into reserves and a fall moves them out. The USGS reassessment is annual and reserves can swing 10 to 20% year-on-year. Second, reserves data for phosphate rock is dominated by one holder (Morocco at c. 70% via the Khouribga / Boucraa basins); treating that share as a share-of-the-world like the others is accurate but hides the fact that the field is close to a monopoly. Third, three minerals are not quoted at all. The tab labels these honestly rather than imputing a share.

Verification pipeline. The Lab's design commits to replace every rounded headline with a BACI-derived value in tonnes of contained metal for tradable stages. The BACI HS6 pipeline uses metal-content coefficients following Nansai et al. (2014) applied to CEPII's harmonised bilateral trade release; that release currently runs one year behind USGS and requires manual download and human review (per scripts/mineral-flows-tools.py baci). Until that refresh runs, the shipped world totals carry the status: indicative flag on the JSON metadata, and every figure surfaced by the tool is labelled with its source in-place. The attribution page lists the primary source and last-updated date for every (mineral, stage) pair; the guide walks through how to read the tonnes alongside the percentages.

Reading the tonnes on the map. In the Overview tab, an unlisted-country "Rest of world" share is included in the world total, so the sum of a stage's listed-country tonnes plus its Rest-of-world tonnes should approximately match the world figure — small discrepancies (a percent or two) reflect the "OTHER" bucket sitting at rounded shares. Where a chain has stage-specific policy in force (a Chinese export licensing rule on high-purity gallium; a Congolese cobalt export tax at the mine gate), the effective tradable tonnes fall short of the map's derived figure. The tool notes those interventions in the Country tab on a per-country basis.

Gaps this map can fill

Most network studies stop at country-level topology and publish static figures; few are interactive, and almost none put the African value-capture question at the centre. The trade-linked MFA literature is methodologically strong but rarely updated after publication.

A maintained, openly sourced tool that joins stage-specific concentration, bilateral flows, dated policy measures and the African processing gap is not something the incumbents offer. It connects directly to the Lab's Green Industrialisation & Local Manufacturing programme.
