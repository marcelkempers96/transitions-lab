Field reading

Assembly to Value: Local Manufacturing in East Africa

What happens when a product moves from being imported to being assembled locally, and whether the "local" part actually reaches the workers, suppliers, and customers it promises to.

The transition in one sentence

Local manufacturing is a claim: that value stays where the thing is made. Whether it does depends on decisions that a shipping manifest cannot see.

Why this reading matters

Governments across East Africa are pushing for local content: buses assembled in-country, motorcycles CKD-imported and put together locally, solar systems packaged with domestically-made brackets. The policy story is straightforward. The lived reality is not. A "locally assembled" vehicle might still have every high-value component imported, with domestic labour capturing only the low-margin final steps. Or it might, quietly, become the first genuinely local supply chain a sector has seen. The difference is not visible in the customs data.

The Lab's reading of local-manufacturing transitions treats the assembly plant as a socio-technical node, not a factory line. Who works there matters. Where the components come from matters. Whether the plant creates a supplier ecosystem or merely relocates the last mile of an import matters. And what the workers, the suppliers, and the customers actually experience matters most of all.

What a case like this asks

Is "local" a label or a value chain?

A vehicle stamped "assembled in country" can still be 90% imported by value. The productive question is not whether assembly happens locally but which steps of the value chain sit inside the country, and where the margins land. Reading a bill of materials tells only half of it; a supplier-network map reads the other half.

Do the workers actually benefit?

Assembly-line jobs are not automatically good jobs. Wage levels, contract stability, skill development, and safety are the measurable dimensions of whether local employment is a real gain or a lower-margin substitute for imports. Only fieldwork with workers and their households can answer this.

Does the ecosystem grow?

A single plant is one datapoint. The transition is a supplier network, or it is not. Whether local component suppliers, tooling shops, and after-sales technicians grow around the plant, or whether every component still ships from a single overseas hub, is what decides whether "local manufacturing" is a durable capability or a subsidy-dependent posture.

What the Lab brings to a case like this

Field research at three levels: workers and their households (Reach and Experience), suppliers and their pricing (Depth of the value-chain claim), and customers on whether local assembly is trusted and preferred (or not). The BRW framework enters at the design stage: is a plant bypassing the incumbent import route, repurposing existing informal-sector capacity, or weakening the case for continued import through visible cost or quality advantages?

For the wider setting, see the Insight on Europe Invents, America Scales, which reads the same "invention vs commercialisation" asymmetry that shapes what a local-assembly economy can actually build.

The wider reading

Local manufacturing is not a single policy question but a chain of them. Where each link sits, and who benefits at each step, is a field question, not an accounting one. The Lab's reading of these transitions starts with the workers, moves to the suppliers, and reaches the customers, and reports what the evidence says, including where the "local" claim breaks down.
