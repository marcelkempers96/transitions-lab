Seven Findings on the Transition-Mineral Chain

Where the minerals in an electric vehicle actually come from, who processes them, and who captures the value. A headline reading of thirty-six studies, from a paper prepared for the Lab's Mineral Flows Map. The full research paper, with sources, is at Where Transition Minerals Go: The Evidence Base.

An electric vehicle is a bill of materials pretending to be a machine. Eight mineral groups go into it before it moves. Four decide the battery alone (4: lithium, nickel, cobalt, graphite). The rest, from steel (1) to copper motor windings (2) to the aluminium body (5) and the silicon in the control boards (8), sit inside the same trade system, with the same pattern: mining is diffuse, refining is not, and the value collects where the refining sits.

A ninth chain matters increasingly and is now on the map: phosphate. Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) cells have overtaken NMC in Chinese EV sales and are expanding globally. The phosphate chain is even more China-concentrated than the battery-metals chains, roughly two-thirds of purified LFP-grade phosphoric acid and about 95% of LFP cathode manufacturing sit in China. Morocco holds the world's largest phosphate rock reserves and is expanding downstream through state-owned OCP; that is the single most watchable state-versus-market move in the transition-mineral chain today.

The paper's conclusion, distilled: the transition-mineral chain is defined by one asymmetry, mining is diffuse, refining is not, and value collects where refining sits. Every headline concentration figure across lithium, cobalt, nickel, rare earths and phosphate moves the same way at the refining stage; no country holds enough of any single mineral to bargain alone; and ownership at the mine is not the same as control at the refinery. The seven findings that follow are what the recent literature makes defensible about that shape. Every numeric reference [1] links back to the source in the research paper.

1. Africa mines the ore and someone else earns the metal.

Africa mines roughly 17% of the world's copper and refines about 9%. Ndubuisi and co-authors find that forty-eight African countries hold reserves of at least one transition mineral, and that Asia, and China in particular, take over 40% of Africa's ore exports. Reserves are not the same as exports at scale: the Mineral Flows Map surfaces the current top African exporters (around a dozen), while the other thirty-plus countries hold deposits that have not yet reached international trade. Congolese cobalt nearly triples in value between the mine gate and local refining, meaning the difference between exporting ore and exporting metal is not a rounding error, it is the price of the smelter. The Ndubuisi conclusion is bleaker than any single number: individual countries supply too little to bargain alone, so the value at stake needs pooled supply, shared processing and common rules, which is a political programme, not a policy line.

2. The concentration is the risk, not any single country.

A 2026 paper in Entropy finds that seven routes carry over 80% of the world's lithium trade value, against 81 for cobalt and 34 for nickel. Read against a decade of network studies in Resources Policy and Resources, Conservation and Recycling, the number of corridors needed to reach the 80% mark is the concentration indicator that actually matters, more than the Herfindahl-Hirschman index the sector reports out of habit. The pattern the multi-layer network studies show for 2010 to 2024 is a chain moving from loosely connected exporters and importers into a tightly coupled small-world graph, which is another way of saying: what happens to any one of those seven lithium routes hits every buyer at once.

3. Country trade maps miss half of it. Ownership maps do not.

Sun and co-authors (2024) built a company-level database of lithium, cobalt, nickel and platinum mining across 240 economies. Foreign direct investment controlled 47% of world lithium output, 71% of cobalt, 41% of nickel and 34% of platinum in 2019. Reallocated to the investor's home country, supply-risk indices change materially, which means the standard "map by mine location" overstates the control that producing countries actually exercise. This is the gap the Ndubuisi paper and the Nordic Africa Institute's 2026 Copperbelt policy note both point at, and it is why the Lab's Mineral Flows Map is being built with an ownership toggle for version 2 rather than one.

4. Export restrictions are rising, but only on the ore end of the chain.

Kowalski and Legendre (2023) used the OECD Inventory of Export Restrictions on Industrial Raw Materials to show a more than fivefold rise in restrictions on critical raw materials since 2009. Every extra restriction is upstream, on ore or concentrate, because WTO rules largely prohibit quantitative export bans; the instruments that remain are taxes and licensing. Andrenelli and colleagues (2025) then track what those restrictions actually buy, on the cobalt, lithium and nickel cases, and find the value gains concentrated in primary processing and often captured by foreign investors, with costs to trading partners and to environmental goals. Restrictions accumulate faster than the processing they were meant to unlock.

5. "Productive" versus "non-productive" processing tells us nothing useful.

The IEA's Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026 reports that the average share of the top refining country reached a record in 2025, that the dominant supplier accounted for nearly all supply growth in manganese, nickel and graphite, and that rare earths were the one chain where refining concentration fell, owing to new capacity in the United States and Malaysia. Read against the four-stage MFA in Resources, Conservation and Recycling, which shows over half of transition-mineral refining occurring in China, the takeaway for a lending or grant-making desk is that "processing" as a category is the wrong resolution. Bauxite (5) refining, manganese (7) refining and battery-metal (4) refining behave nothing like each other; putting them on the same slide hides the only variables that decide who captures value.

6. The mining stage is diffuse. The failures cluster elsewhere.

The four-stage MFA reports about 69% of cobalt mined in the DRC and 80% of lithium mined in Australia and Chile, which sounds like the mining stage is the concentrated one. It is not. Nine countries account for the majority of every early-stage mineral, but the ownership, refining and cathode stages compress that further, and the environmental and social risk sits mostly at the mining end: Lebre et al. (2020) found cobalt in high-risk contexts across their seven-dimension ESG matrix, and Owen et al. (2023) showed most transition-mineral projects sit on or near land held by Indigenous and peasant peoples. A country-level map coloured by production share hides both facts. The Lab's ESG overlay for version 2 of the map is where they surface.

7. Recycled batteries could meet a serious share of demand by 2040. Under conditions almost nothing meets today.

Zeng et al. (2022) and Dunn et al. (2021), running dynamic material-flow models with stocks, retirement and recycling, find that under idealised recovery, retired batteries could meet about 60% of cobalt and 53% of lithium and nickel demand by 2040. Read that number as a ceiling rather than a forecast: it requires idealised collection, near-complete recovery, and refining capacity in the geographies where end-of-life batteries actually arrive. None of those conditions holds today. The finding matters not because recycling is close, but because the ceiling exists at all: any transition-mineral policy that ignores end-of-life is choosing to leave that ceiling below its intended demand. For the map, that is the case for a recycling stage in version 2, and the case for measuring the collection rate as a distinct policy indicator.

What this piece deliberately does not say

Three claims that a reader might expect are not made here, because the evidence is not there yet.

First, no headline number for "African refining capacity is on track to close the gap by year X". The literature that supports such a projection does not exist in a form that survives review; the number that does exist is the one already in the piece, which is the mine-to-refine share ratio today.

Second, no attribution of a specific price shock to a specific chokepoint. Network robustness studies model plausibly what a lost route would do, but the identified counterfactuals are simulations, not observed events.

Third, no forecast for lithium price to 2030. This piece is a reading of the chain's shape; the chain's shape does not tell you the price. The concentration numbers tell you the price's volatility, which is a different question.
